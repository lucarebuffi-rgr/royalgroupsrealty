House Not Selling? What to Do After Your Listing Expires

By Luca Rebuffi – Royal Groups Realty  ·  November 2026  ·  8 min read
The Reality

A Listing That Doesn’t Sell Doesn’t Mean You Failed

You did what you were supposed to do. You hired an agent, cleaned the house, kept it showing-ready for weeks, and waited. Now the sign is still in the yard, the listing has expired or is about to, and every month of silence is another month of mortgage, taxes, insurance, and utilities on a house you wanted to be finished with.

It happens to a lot of sellers, and it’s rarely about effort. A house that sits is usually telling you one of a few things: the price doesn’t match what buyers can finance, the condition is scaring off buyers or lenders, or the listing has been up so long that buyers assume something is wrong with it. The longer it sits, the harder each of those gets to fix.

The good news is that a listing that didn’t sell is not a dead end. We buy houses after listings expire, and after price drops, throughout Texas and Georgia — as-is, for cash, with no repairs, no showings, and no commissions.

💡 Before you talk to any buyer — including us — pull out your listing agreement. It controls what you can do and when, and we explain what to look for below.

Why Houses Sit

Why Some Houses Don’t Sell

When a house lingers on the market, it’s almost always one of these:

  • Price. Priced above what recent comparable sales support, or above what a buyer’s appraisal will back up. Overpriced listings tend to sit while better-priced homes nearby go under contract.
  • Condition. Dated systems, deferred maintenance, a roof near the end of its life, foundation or water problems — issues an inspection will find and a lender may not finance.
  • Financing standards. Most retail buyers use FHA, VA, or conventional loans, and each has minimum property standards. A house that doesn’t meet them narrows the buyer pool to people who can pay cash.
  • A stale listing. Buyers can see how long a house has been on the market. After enough weeks, “what’s wrong with it?” becomes the first question, and price cuts can start to look like desperation.
  • Deals that fall through. A contract that collapses over financing or an inspection resets the clock and can leave the listing marked as back on the market.

What waiting actually costs

Every month a house sits has a price tag, even when it doesn’t show up as a bill. Mortgage payments, property taxes, insurance, utilities, and lawn care all keep running. As an illustration, if holding the house costs $1,800 a month, four more months on the market is $7,200 — before any price reductions. Vacant homes can also pick up extra problems, like insurance limits and maintenance issues no one is around to catch.

Then there are the costs on the sale itself: roughly 6% in agent commissions on a listed home, plus whatever repairs a buyer asks for after the inspection. A lower cash number that closes in days can leave you with more than a higher listing price that closes in months, or never. Your Options

What to Do After a Listing Expires

Option 1: Relist with a new price or a new agent

This can work if the problem is mainly price, you’re not in a hurry, and you can keep carrying the house. Ask any agent you interview for the recent comparable sales that justify their number, and what they would do differently this time. Relisting without changing the price, the condition, or the marketing usually brings the same result.

Option 2: Fix it up and relist

If specific repairs are what’s blocking buyers and lenders, fixing them can widen the buyer pool. The catch is cost and time: you pay for the work up front, wait for it to finish, then start another listing clock. Get written quotes before you commit, and compare the total against what the repairs are likely to add to the sale price.

Option 3: Rent it out

Renting turns the house into an income property, but it also makes you a landlord, with tenant screening, repairs, and vacancies. If your goal was to be finished with the house, renting usually just changes the kind of work you’re doing. Our guide for tired landlords covers what that can look like.

Option 4: Sell for cash

A cash sale makes sense when you want a fixed outcome and a fixed date: no showings, no repairs, no buyer financing to fall through, and no commissions. The trade-off is price. A cash offer will usually come in below a full retail price — typically 70% to 85% of fair market value, depending on condition — so the real question is what you net after commissions, repairs, and months of carrying costs. Check your listing agreement first

A listing agreement is a contract. Some expire cleanly on a set date, while others include clauses that can affect a sale after the term ends, such as a protection period for buyers the agent introduced. Read yours, or ask your agent for a written release if you want out early. We can’t give legal advice, but we would rather you know your obligations before you sign anything with anyone.

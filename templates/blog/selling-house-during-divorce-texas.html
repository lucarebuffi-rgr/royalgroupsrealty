Selling a House During Divorce in Texas

By Luca Rebuffi – Royal Groups Realty  ·  March 2026  ·  10 min read
The Reality

Selling During Divorce Is Emotional — The Transaction Doesn't Have to Be

Selling a home during divorce is one of the most emotionally and logistically complex situations a homeowner can face. The house is often the largest asset in the marriage, the place where your family built memories, and the last remaining financial tie between two people trying to separate their lives.

In Texas — a community property state — both spouses typically have equal legal ownership of the marital home, which means both must agree before anything can happen. That requirement alone creates conflict in a situation that is already under enormous strain.

This guide covers what you actually need to know: how Texas community property law applies to your home, what happens when spouses disagree, what a Texas divorce timeline really looks like, the tax angle most people miss, and why a fast cash sale is often the most financially rational path — not just the easiest emotionally.

The Law

Texas Community Property Law and Your Home

Texas is one of nine community property states in the U.S. The core principle: property acquired during the marriage is owned equally by both spouses, regardless of whose income paid for it or whose name is on the deed or mortgage. This creates specific rules around selling the marital home.

  • Both spouses must agree to sell — a unilateral decision by one spouse to sell is not legally effective
  • Both spouses must sign the deed at closing — though this can happen at separate times or remotely
  • Proceeds are typically split equally (50/50) unless a court orders a different division
  • Separate property claims can change the math — see below

Community Property vs. Separate Property

Not every home is automatically community property. The distinction matters for how proceeds are divided:

Community Property

  • Home purchased during the marriage
  • Even if mortgage is in one name only
  • Even if one spouse paid more toward it
  • Even if one spouse did all the repairs
  • Split: typically 50/50 at divorce

Separate Property

  • Home owned before the marriage
  • Home inherited during the marriage
  • Home received as a gift during marriage
  • Must be clearly documented to claim
  • Split: may go entirely to one spouse

Separate property claims are contested in a significant percentage of Texas divorces. The burden of proof is on the spouse making the claim, and documentation matters. If the separate property home was later refinanced jointly, or if community funds were used for major renovations, the separate property character may be partially or fully lost. This is a matter for your divorce attorney — not something to guess about.

📌 Mortgage is only in one name? Doesn’t matter. Under Texas community property law, if the home was purchased during the marriage, both spouses still must sign the deed. The mortgage payoff comes from closing proceeds regardless of whose name it’s in.

The Timeline

What a Texas Divorce Actually Looks Like, Timeline-Wise

Texas law imposes a 60-day mandatory waiting period after filing before a divorce can be finalized. That is the minimum possible. The reality for most divorces involving a home and contested assets is significantly longer:

1

Filing & Service (Week 1–3)

One spouse files a petition for divorce in the county where you live (Tarrant, Harris, Dallas, Bexar, etc.). The other spouse is formally served. A temporary orders hearing may be requested to establish who stays in the home during the process.

2

Temporary Orders (Week 2–6)

If contested, the court may issue temporary orders about the marital home — who can live there, who pays the mortgage, whether it can be listed for sale. These orders govern until the final decree. This is when to get your cash offer so you have a concrete number to bring to negotiations.

3

Discovery & Negotiation (Month 2–6+)

Both sides exchange financial disclosures, valuations, and documentation. The home’s value is typically established through an appraisal. Negotiation on how to divide the home (buy-out vs. sale vs. partition) happens here.

4

Settlement or Trial (Month 3–12+)

About 95% of Texas divorces settle before trial. If the home sale is part of the settlement, it can be executed as part of the final decree. Contested divorces going to trial average 12–18 months.

5

Final Decree & Home Sale

The court signs the final decree. If the decree orders the home sold, it can close immediately. Many couples choose to close the home sale before the final decree to simplify the remaining settlement.

Selling the home early — before the divorce is final — often shortens the total divorce timeline by eliminating the largest source of negotiation conflict. With the home sold and proceeds distributed, there is simply less to fight over.

When One Refuses

What If One Spouse Refuses to Sell?

This is the most common complication in divorce-related home sales. If one spouse refuses to agree to a sale, the other can petition the court for a partition order — a court directive forcing the sale of jointly owned property.

Texas courts regularly grant partition orders when both of the following are true: (1) the property cannot be practically divided between the spouses (a house cannot be split in half), and (2) one spouse’s refusal is delaying financial resolution. Judges understand that keeping two estranged people financially tied to a single asset prolongs the conflict and costs both parties money.

The reality of a partition action:

  • Adds 3–6 months to the process minimum
  • Attorney fees of $3,000–$8,000+ per side, often more in contested cases
  • Increased emotional conflict and court appearances
  • Eventual sale that often nets less than a negotiated sale because it goes through a court-appointed receiver

A concrete cash offer on the table often breaks the stalemate faster than any legal threat. When one spouse refuses to sell at $400K but sees a written offer for $335K in 21 days, the math becomes real. The question changes from “do we sell?” to “how much does waiting cost us?”

The Tax Question

Capital Gains and the $500K Exclusion — What Divorce Does to It

Most homeowners know about the federal capital gains exclusion on a primary residence: $250,000 for single filers, $500,000 for married filing jointly. But divorce changes the equation in ways most people don’t consider until it’s too late.

To qualify for the full $500K married exclusion, the sale must occur while you are still legally married. Once the divorce is final, each spouse is a single filer with a $250K cap. For a home that has appreciated significantly — bought in 2010 for $200K, worth $550K today — the difference in taxes is real:

  • Sell before divorce is final: $350K gain − $500K exclusion = $0 capital gains tax
  • Sell after divorce (each spouse): $175K gain per person − $250K exclusion each = still $0 (in this scenario)
  • Higher-appreciation scenario ($600K gain): pre-divorce = $100K taxable; post-divorce each spouse may have $175K taxable at ~15–20% federal

The two-year ownership and use test must also be satisfied (lived in the home for at least 2 of the last 5 years). If one spouse moved out more than two years ago, their exclusion eligibility may be reduced. Texas has no state income tax, so only the federal rate applies.

Bottom line: If your home has appreciated significantly, talk to a CPA before the divorce is finalized. Timing the sale relative to the divorce can make a material difference. This is not tax advice — always consult a professional for your specific situation.

Liens & Mortgage

Liens, Mortgage Payoff, and Underwater Situations

Any outstanding mortgage must be paid off at closing through the title company — this happens automatically. If there are additional liens on the property (judgment liens, tax liens, mechanic’s liens, HOA liens), those are also resolved through the title company from the closing proceeds. The estate’s attorneys don’t need to handle these separately.

If judgment liens were filed against one spouse — such as from a lawsuit or unpaid debt — those liens may attach to the community property interest. The title company identifies them in the title search and they are paid at closing.

If the home is underwater (liens exceed the home’s value), a short sale may be an option. We can discuss the specifics of your situation. Read more: Selling a House With Liens.

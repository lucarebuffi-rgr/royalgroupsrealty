Know Your Timeline

How the Tax Foreclosure Process Works in Texas and Georgia

The two states operate very differently. Here is a plain-language breakdown of exactly what happens if you do nothing:

🏫 Texas Tax Foreclosure Timeline

  1. February 1: Taxes become delinquent for the prior tax year. Penalty of 6% plus 1% per month begins accruing.
  2. July 1: An additional 15–20% collection attorney fee is added in most Texas counties. Total penalty can reach 42–47% by mid-year.
  3. Year 1–2: The taxing unit can file suit to foreclose the tax lien. Most counties wait 1–2 years before filing.
  4. After judgment: A court-ordered sale can happen within 60–90 days of the judge’s ruling. At that point, the property is auctioned on the courthouse steps.
  5. After sale: Former owners have a 2-year right of redemption on homestead property (180 days on investment property) — but must pay the full sale price plus 25% premium.

🏫 Georgia Tax Foreclosure Timeline

  1. January 1: Property taxes assessed. Due by December 20 of that year in most Georgia counties.
  2. After 90 days delinquent: County can issue a FIFA (tax lien). Penalties, interest, and fees begin adding up rapidly.
  3. 12 months after FIFA: County can initiate a tax deed sale. Fulton, Gwinnett, DeKalb, and Cobb Counties actively pursue delinquent properties.
  4. At tax deed sale: Property is sold to the highest bidder. Owner has a 12-month right of redemption — but must pay full purchase price plus 20% premium.
  5. After 12-month redemption: New owner can petition to quiet title. Former owner has no further claim.

The key takeaway: in both states, the total amount you owe grows every month due to penalties, interest, and attorney fees. A $12,000 delinquent tax balance can easily become $18,000–$22,000 by the time a sale is scheduled. Selling sooner protects more of your equity.

State-by-State Detail

Texas: The Hidden Cost of Waiting

Texas has some of the highest property tax rates in the country — effective rates typically run 1.6%–2.5% depending on the county and school district. That means a $200,000 home in Dallas County or Tarrant County can generate $4,000–$5,000 in annual taxes. Fall two years behind and you’re looking at $8,000–$10,000 in principal alone — before penalties.

Texas law adds a 6% penalty in February when taxes become delinquent, then 1% per month through June. On July 1, most taxing authorities add a 15%–20% collection attorney fee on top of everything already owed. By July of the first delinquent year, a $5,000 tax bill has become $6,750–$7,250. By year two, with the balance compounding, the total payoff can be nearly double the original tax amount.

High-volume Texas counties — Harris, Dallas, Tarrant, Collin, Bexar — file suit faster than rural counties and have dedicated tax collections departments. Waiting “one more month” is rarely worth it.

Georgia: Tax Deed Sales Are Faster Than You Think

Georgia’s property tax rates are lower than Texas — most Metro Atlanta counties run 0.75%–1.2% effective rates — but the state’s tax deed process is aggressive. Once a FIFA (tax lien) is issued, the county can initiate a tax deed sale in as little as 12 months. And unlike Texas, Georgia’s right of redemption is only 12 months — not 2 years.

The Metro Atlanta counties — Fulton, Gwinnett, DeKalb, Cobb, Clayton — conduct regular tax deed auctions and are not known for leniency. Rural Georgia counties may move more slowly, but once the process starts, it moves fast.

Georgia also adds a 1% per month interest charge on delinquent taxes plus a 20% redemption premium if a third party buys the tax deed. If someone buys the tax deed on your property, you must pay them the full purchase price plus 20% to get your property back — in addition to the original taxes owed.

Common Combinations

Delinquent Taxes Rarely Come Alone

Most homeowners who reach out about delinquent taxes are also dealing with one or more of these situations. All of them can be resolved through the same closing:

Delinquent Taxes + Mortgage Foreclosure

The most common combination. If you’re behind on both your mortgage and your property taxes, the lender’s foreclosure process and the county’s tax lien process are running simultaneously — and they can collide in unpredictable ways. A cash sale closes both. Read: How to Avoid Foreclosure in Texas.

Delinquent Taxes + IRS Federal Tax Lien

IRS tax liens attach to real estate and show up in a title search. They are subordinate to property tax liens in most states but must be satisfied at closing. The title company handles IRS lien payoffs directly — you don’t need to call the IRS. Read: Selling a House With Liens.

Delinquent Taxes + Inherited Property

Extremely common — a parent passes away, the estate goes through probate, and during the 6–12 month process, the property taxes accumulate. By the time heirs can legally sell, there are 1–2 years of back taxes sitting on the property. We handle this regularly in both Texas and Georgia. Read: Selling an Inherited Home in Georgia.

Delinquent Taxes + Financial Hardship or Bankruptcy

Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 bankruptcy may temporarily delay a tax sale through the automatic stay, but it does not eliminate property tax debt. A cash sale while in bankruptcy requires trustee approval but is often the cleanest path forward. Read: Selling During Financial Hardship & Bankruptcy.

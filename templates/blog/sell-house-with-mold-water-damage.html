How to Sell a House With Mold or Water Damage

By Luca Rebuffi – Royal Groups Realty  ·  October 2026  ·  9 min read
The Reality

Mold and Water Damage Don’t Have to Stop Your Sale

Water always finds a way in. A slow roof leak over a bedroom, a supply line that failed inside a wall, a slab leak nobody noticed, an AC drain line that backed up for a whole summer, or a storm that pushed an inch of water across the first floor. By the time the damage is visible — a brown ceiling stain, a musty smell, black spotting in a closet — it has usually spread well past what you can see. For homeowners in Texas and Georgia, especially in humid Gulf Coast and coastal markets, it’s one of the most stressful problems a house can have.

It’s also one of the hardest to sell around the traditional way. Buyers get nervous at the word “mold,” inspectors write it up in bold, and lenders and appraisers commonly flag active leaks, visible mold, and water-damaged ceilings or floors before they’ll approve a loan. A house that could have sold quickly a year ago can sit for months once the damage is on paper.

We buy houses with mold, water damage, and flood damage throughout Texas and Georgia — including humid markets like Harris County, Jefferson County, and Orange County — as-is, for cash, with no repairs, no remediation, and no commissions. You don’t need to tear out drywall, hire a remediation crew, or get the house “good enough to list” first.

💡 Even in an as-is cash sale, be upfront about what you know. Telling us about the leak, the repair history, and any mold reports isn’t a weakness in negotiation — it’s how we build an accurate offer, and it protects you legally.

Why It’s Hard

Why Water-Damaged Houses Are So Hard to List

A traditional sale depends on a buyer using a mortgage, and a mortgage depends on an appraisal and an inspection. That’s exactly where water damage causes trouble:

  • Appraisal and lender flags. Appraisers note active leaks, stained ceilings, and visible mold, and FHA and VA loans in particular can require the problem to be fixed before they’ll close.
  • Inspection renegotiation. Even a willing buyer will often reopen the price or walk away after an inspector documents moisture or mold.
  • Insurance problems. Past water claims can show up in insurance claims-history reports, and some insurers decline or surcharge a house with a recent water loss — something a buyer has to sort out before closing.
  • Hidden scope. Visible damage is often the tip of it. Wet insulation, a rotted subfloor, damaged ductwork, and moisture inside the framing can turn a “small” leak into a major repair bill.
  • Time. Moisture damage gets worse the longer it sits, which is why waiting for the right retail buyer usually costs more than people expect.

Signs the damage is bigger than it looks

If you’re trying to decide whether this is a small cleanup or a major problem, these are the signs that usually point to the bigger kind:

  • A musty smell that comes back after you clean or run a dehumidifier
  • Ceiling stains that return after being painted over
  • Soft, spongy, or warped flooring, or baseboards pulling away from the wall
  • Bubbling or peeling paint and wallpaper on interior walls
  • Mold in more than one room, or around vents and the HVAC system
  • A leak whose source hasn’t actually been found and fixed

Any one of these can be manageable. Several together usually mean the cost to make the house retail-ready is high, and that is when selling as-is to a cash buyer tends to make the most financial sense. Water damage that overlaps with other problems — foundation issues or storm and fire damage — compounds quickly.

Before You Sell

Disclosure, Remediation, and Your Options

What Texas sellers should know

Texas has specific rules around mold. Mold work covering 25 or more contiguous square feet generally has to be done by a state-licensed assessor and remediator, and when a licensed project is finished, the assessor issues a Certificate of Mold Remediation. Under Texas Occupations Code Section 1958.154, a seller must give the buyer a copy of each remediation certificate issued for the property in the five years before the sale. Texas also requires most residential sellers to deliver a written Seller’s Disclosure Notice, which asks about known water penetration and flooding. Homeowners cleaning up their own property are generally exempt from the licensing rules, but the disclosure obligations don’t go away.

What Georgia sellers should know

Georgia doesn’t use one statutory disclosure form the way Texas does, and it’s a “buyer beware” state — but that doesn’t mean sellers can stay quiet. Georgia sellers have a duty to disclose known latent defects, meaning problems a buyer couldn’t reasonably spot, and to answer a buyer’s direct questions honestly. Hidden water damage or mold that a seller knows about is exactly the kind of problem that creates legal exposure if it’s concealed, and an “as-is” clause generally doesn’t protect a seller who actively hides a known issue.

Should you remediate before selling?

Sometimes, but often not. Professional remediation can run from a few hundred dollars for a small, contained area to many thousands once the problem is inside walls, subfloors, or ductwork — and that’s before the repair work (drywall, flooring, paint, carpentry) needed to make the house look sellable again. If the house then sells on the retail market, you pay commissions on top of all of it.

Remediating makes the most sense when the damage is small and contained, the source of the water is fixed, you have time, and the house is otherwise in solid retail condition. When the damage is widespread, the leak is unresolved, the house is vacant, or you’re dealing with an estate, a deadline, or a tenant situation, selling as-is usually leaves more money in your pocket than a renovation you have to finance up front. What a cash offer looks like on a damaged house

Our offers are built from the house’s value after repairs, minus the cost of the remediation and repair work it needs. That means a house with significant mold or water damage will come in lower than the same house in good condition — that’s the honest reality, and we’ll show you the math rather than hand you a number. What you’re really comparing is our offer against the true net from a listing: remediation, repairs, commissions, carrying costs, and the risk the deal falls through.

How to Sell a House With a Reverse Mortgage

By Luca Rebuffi – Royal Groups Realty  ·  October 2026  ·  9 min read
The Reality

A Reverse Mortgage Doesn’t Stop You From Selling

A reverse mortgage lets homeowners 62 and older borrow against the equity in their home without making monthly mortgage payments. For many families it was the right tool at the right time. But eventually the loan comes due, and that moment tends to arrive during a hard season of life: a parent has passed away, a loved one is moving into care, or the homeowner can no longer keep up with taxes, insurance, and repairs.

The good news is that a house with a reverse mortgage can be sold like any other house. The loan is paid off from the sale proceeds at closing, and whatever equity is left belongs to the owner or the estate. The challenge isn’t whether you can sell — it’s that the servicer’s clock is running, interest keeps adding to the balance, and the house often needs more work than the family has time or money to put into it.

We buy houses with reverse mortgages throughout Texas and Georgia — as-is, for cash, with no repairs, no commissions, and a closing date that fits inside the servicer’s deadline. If you’re working through an estate, our guides to selling an inherited home in Texas and in Georgia cover the rest of the process.

⏰ If you’ve received a due-and-payable notice, don’t wait. The response window is short, and servicers are generally much more flexible with families who stay in contact and show progress.

How the Clock Works

When a Reverse Mortgage Comes Due

The most common type of reverse mortgage is a Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, or HECM, which is insured by the FHA. A HECM generally becomes due and payable when:

  • The last surviving borrower passes away
  • The borrower sells the home or no longer lives in it as a primary residence, including a long-term move into assisted living or a nursing facility
  • The borrower falls behind on property taxes or homeowners insurance, or doesn’t keep the home in reasonable repair

Throughout all of this, interest and mortgage insurance premiums keep accruing, so the balance grows every month the home sits.

The timeline after a borrower passes away

Once the servicer is notified of a death, it sends a due-and-payable notice. The general framework for a HECM looks like this, though every servicer and situation is a little different:

  1. About 30 days to respond. Heirs generally have 30 days from the notice to tell the servicer what they plan to do.
  2. Up to 6 months to resolve it. Heirs can sell the home, pay off the loan, or turn the property over to the lender.
  3. Possible extensions. Two additional 90-day extensions may be available with HUD approval if you can show you’re making progress, such as a signed contract or an active estate case.

That adds up to as much as a year in the best case, but it isn’t automatic, and extensions typically depend on documenting that the sale is moving forward. Other types of reverse mortgages, including private “proprietary” loans, can follow different rules, so confirm the specifics with the servicer. Surviving spouses may also have additional protections depending on the loan, so raise that with the servicer and an attorney right away.

Your Options

What Owners and Heirs Can Actually Do

If the house is worth more than the loan

This is the simplest case. The house sells, the reverse mortgage is paid off at closing, and the remaining equity is yours or your estate’s. For an estate, that money passes to the heirs as part of the normal probate process. The goal in this situation is simply to sell at a fair price before the growing balance eats into the equity.

If the loan is worth more than the house

This is where families worry most, and where the HECM rules help. These loans are non-recourse, which means heirs are generally not personally responsible for more than the home is worth. Heirs can resolve the loan by paying the lesser of the balance or 95% of the home’s appraised value, and FHA insurance covers the shortfall. In some cases, heirs can also hand the property back to the lender with a deed in lieu of foreclosure.

It’s worth being honest about one limit: because the 95% figure is based on an appraisal, a house in rough condition may appraise well above what a cash buyer can pay for it. If that’s your situation, we’ll tell you upfront whether a sale works or whether another route is a better fit to discuss with your attorney. We would rather give you a straight answer than waste your deadline.

If heirs want to keep the home

Heirs who want to keep the property can pay off the loan, usually by refinancing into a traditional mortgage or using other funds. The same 95% rule applies, which can make the payoff lower than the full balance. If you’re comparing paths, our guide to what happens to a mortgage when you sell for cash explains how any payoff works at the closing table.

Where a cash sale fits

A cash sale makes the most sense when the house needs work, the estate or the family can’t afford to carry it, there are several heirs who don’t want the burden of managing a sale, or the servicer’s deadline is approaching. What to do first

Start with the servicer. Ask for a payoff statement, confirm the deadline in writing, and ask what documents they need to approve an extension. If the borrower has passed away, work with the estate’s attorney to confirm who has legal authority to sell — our probate real estate guide explains how that works in Texas and Georgia. Then send us the address and the payoff amount, and we’ll give you an offer within 24 hours.

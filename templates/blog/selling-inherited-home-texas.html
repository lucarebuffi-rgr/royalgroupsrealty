Selling an Inherited Home in Texas

The Situation

Inheriting a Home Is Complicated. Selling It Doesn’t Have to Be.

Inheriting a home in Texas is rarely as simple as “now you own a house.” There’s probate to work through. Property taxes still accumulating every month. Utilities that need to keep running. Possibly a mortgage that’s still active. Family members with different opinions on what to do. And — often — a lifetime of belongings inside that nobody knows what to do with.

Texas Probate

How Probate Works in Texas — and Why It’s Faster Here

Texas is one of the more probate-friendly states in the country. Most estates go through independent administration, which is significantly faster and cheaper than the “dependent” administration required in many other states. Understanding which path applies to your situation determines how quickly you can sell.

Path 1: Independent Administration (Most Common)

If the will names an independent executor — or if the heirs agree to it — Texas allows the executor to manage and sell estate assets without seeking court approval at every step. Letters Testamentary are typically issued within 4–8 weeks of filing, and once issued, the executor can usually contract to sell the property immediately. The sale closes before probate fully wraps up.

Path 2: Affidavit of Heirship (No Formal Probate)

If the deceased left no will and the estate is straightforward, Texas allows title to transfer through a sworn Affidavit of Heirship — a document signed by disinterested witnesses who know the family history, then filed with the county clerk. This avoids the probate court entirely for the purpose of transferring and selling real estate. It’s faster and cheaper than formal probate but requires a title company willing to insure it — we work with title companies experienced with this process.

Path 3: Small Estate Affidavit

For estates worth less than $75,000 (excluding homestead and exempt property), Texas allows a simplified Small Estate Affidavit process. This can allow an heir to sell the property without opening a full probate case.

Path 4: Muniment of Title

If the deceased left a valid will, there are no debts (other than a mortgage), and no other reason to appoint an executor, Texas courts can admit the will as a “muniment of title” — a court order that itself transfers property rights without appointing an executor. This is a relatively quick court process and commonly used in Texas for inherited properties when the estate is otherwise settled.

💡 Texas is one of the fastest probate states. Independent administration means no court approval for every action. Affidavit of Heirship and muniment of title options can bypass formal probate entirely for straightforward situations. In most cases, the ability to sell comes within 4–10 weeks of the owner’s passing — not the 12–18 months families sometimes expect.

Probate Courts by County

In Texas, probate is handled at the county court level. For large counties, there are dedicated statutory probate courts with experienced judges who move cases efficiently. The major markets we serve:

Dallas County

Harris County (Houston)

Rural Counties

Smaller counties use county courts at law for probate. Timelines vary, but Affidavit of Heirship is especially common for rural land and ranch properties.

The Tax Question

Taxes on Inherited Homes in Texas: The Good News

Texas is one of the most tax-friendly states in the country for inherited property. Here’s the full picture.

State-level taxes: essentially zero

  • No state income tax — Texas doesn’t tax your sale proceeds at all.
  • No state estate tax — unlike some states, Texas doesn’t tax the estate itself at death.
  • No state inheritance tax — you owe nothing to the state of Texas for receiving the property.

Federal capital gains: usually zero thanks to the stepped-up basis

Here’s the most important part most families don’t know. When someone dies, the IRS resets the “cost basis” of inherited property to its fair market value on the date of death. This is called a stepped-up basis.

Example: Your parent bought a Dallas home in 1988 for $75,000. It’s worth $340,000 when they pass. You inherit it. Your new cost basis is $340,000 — not $75,000. If you sell within a few months for $340,000, your capital gain is effectively $0 and you owe no federal capital gains tax.

For families who inherited homes in rapidly appreciating Texas markets — Austin, Dallas, Houston — this stepped-up basis can represent tens of thousands of dollars in avoided taxes compared to if the original owner had sold before passing.

The one that catches families off guard: property taxes keep accumulating

Property taxes in Texas are among the highest in the country — often 2.0–2.5% of assessed value per year. On a $275K home, that’s $5,500–$6,875 per year, or roughly $460–$575 per month, accumulating from the day the previous owner passes until the property is sold. The longer probate drags or the family delays a decision, the more this drains the estate.

Add utilities, insurance (if the policy survives or a new one is obtained), and basic maintenance — and an inherited Texas home can cost the estate $600–$900+ per month while vacant. This is one of the primary reasons families choose a fast cash sale over a 60–90 day MLS listing.

📌 Delinquent property taxes are resolved at closing through the title company — they come out of sale proceeds, not your pocket before closing. If the estate has accumulated tax debt, it doesn’t prevent a sale; it’s just paid from proceeds. Read: Selling a Home With Delinquent Property Taxes.

What You Actually Net: Cash Sale vs. MLS Listing

The comparison between cash and MLS isn’t just about price — it’s about total dollars delivered to the estate after every cost. And critically: for many estates, the MLS path isn’t realistic at all because the estate can’t front the repair and prep costs.

Common Complications

Inherited Property Situations We Handle Every Week

Multiple Heirs Who Don’t All Agree

This is one of the most common situations in inherited property. One heir wants to sell quickly. Another wants to keep it. A third lives in the home and doesn’t want to leave. Options include: buying out the holdout’s share, negotiating a compromise with a defined timeline, or — in extreme cases — a court partition action. We can also purchase just the willing heirs’ interests in some situations. Read: Selling a Partial Interest in a Property.

The Estate Has No Cash for Repairs

Many inherited homes are owned free and clear but the estate itself has no liquid assets — no savings to fund a $15,000–$30,000 renovation, staging, and carrying costs. For these estates, the MLS path simply isn’t available, even if it would theoretically produce a higher gross price. A cash sale at as-is value is the only practical option.

The Home Has Been Vacant for Years

Deferred maintenance accelerates when a home sits vacant. Roof leaks, HVAC failures, foundation issues, pest infestations, mold — every month vacant increases the repair scope. We buy homes in any state of disrepair. No pre-listing repairs, no cleaning. Read: Selling a House As-Is in DFW.

Title Complications or Unknown Liens

Inherited properties sometimes carry title clouds — mechanics liens, judgment liens, unpaid HOA assessments, or even previous owners whose interests were never properly cleared. The title company performs a full search, and known liens are resolved at closing from sale proceeds. We don’t require clean title before accepting a contract — we work with the title company to get it there. Read: Selling a House With Liens.

Hoarder Situations

Some inherited homes contain decades of accumulation — furniture, collections, personal items filling every room. We buy hoarder properties as-is. Leave everything. We coordinate cleanout after closing. Read: Selling a Hoarder House.

Selling an Inherited Home in Texas — Probate, Taxes & What You Actually Net

By Luca Rebuffi – Royal Groups Realty  ·  June 2025  ·  9 min read

Inheriting a home is rarely a clean, simple event. Even when the inheritance itself is straightforward, the property usually comes with a tangle of questions: Do you need to go through probate? What taxes apply? Should you fix it up or sell as-is? What if there are multiple heirs who don't agree? And — most importantly — what will you actually walk away with after all the dust settles?

This guide walks through everything you need to know about selling an inherited home in Texas. Whether the property is in Dallas, Houston, Austin, San Antonio, or a small county hundreds of miles from the nearest metro, the rules and the math work the same way.

First — Do You Have to Go Through Probate?

In Texas, most inherited homes have to pass through some form of probate before they can be sold. Probate is the legal process that transfers ownership from the deceased person's estate to the heirs. The good news: Texas has one of the most streamlined probate systems in the country.

The Three Main Probate Paths in Texas

  • Independent administration — the most common path. If the will names an executor or all heirs agree, the court appoints an executor who can sell the property without ongoing court supervision. Typically takes 30–90 days to get authority to sell.
  • Muniment of title — a fast-track option when there's a will, no debts beyond a mortgage, and the only asset is real estate. Often resolved in 30–60 days and dramatically cheaper than full probate.
  • Affidavit of heirship — used when there's no will and the heirs agree. Doesn't require a court proceeding at all. Heirs sign a sworn statement; title companies often accept this for sale purposes after a brief waiting period.

💡 Important: You don't have to wait until probate fully closes to start the sale process. We routinely sign purchase agreements during probate and close once the executor or heirs have authority to sign the deed. This can shave weeks off your timeline.

What Taxes Apply When You Sell an Inherited Home in Texas?

This is the question that worries most heirs — and the answer is usually better than people expect.

Federal Estate Tax

For 2025, the federal estate tax exemption is over $13.6 million per individual. Unless the entire estate is worth more than that, no federal estate tax is owed. The overwhelming majority of inherited Texas homes are nowhere near this threshold.

Texas Estate Tax

Texas has no state estate tax and no state inheritance tax. None. You don't owe Texas a dime simply because you inherited the property.

Capital Gains Tax — The "Stepped-Up Basis" That Saves You Money

Here's where most heirs are pleasantly surprised. When you inherit a home, the IRS treats your "basis" (the starting value for calculating capital gains) as the home's fair market value on the date of death — NOT what the original owner paid for it decades ago.

Example: Your parents bought a house in 1985 for $60,000. When the surviving parent passed away in 2025, it was worth $310,000. If you sell it shortly after for $310,000, your taxable capital gain is $0 — even though the house appreciated by $250,000 over 40 years. That's the stepped-up basis at work.

If the home keeps appreciating after you inherit it, you only owe capital gains tax on the increase since the date of death. A quick sale typically means little to no capital gains exposure.

Property Taxes

Texas has high property taxes (around 1.6–2.5% depending on the county). If the deceased had a homestead exemption or "over-65" tax freeze, you may lose those when ownership transfers — which means the annual tax bill jumps. This is one of the biggest hidden costs of holding an inherited Texas home.

None of this is tax advice — every estate has its own quirks, and you should always confirm specifics with a CPA or estate attorney. But the general framework above applies to the overwhelming majority of inherited Texas homes.

The Real Cost of Holding an Inherited Texas Home

One of the most common mistakes heirs make is underestimating what it costs to simply hold an inherited property while they figure out what to do with it. Here's what a typical Texas inherited home costs you every month:

  • Property taxes: $400–$1,200/month depending on location and lost exemptions
  • Insurance: $150–$300/month (and rates spike for vacant homes)
  • Utilities: $100–$250/month even if no one's living there
  • HOA dues (if applicable): $50–$400/month
  • Maintenance & lawn care: $100–$300/month
  • Mortgage (if any remains): Whatever the deceased was paying

That's easily $1,000–$3,000+ per month before any unexpected repairs. Drag the sale out by six months and you've spent $6,000–$18,000 keeping a house you don't live in.

Your Three Options for Selling an Inherited Texas Home

Option 1: List With a Realtor After Renovating

If the home is in good condition and you have the time and money to prep it, listing on the MLS can net the highest sale price. But factor in:

  • Repairs and updates (often $15K–$50K to make an older Texas home competitive)
  • Agent commissions (5–6%)
  • 2–4 months of holding costs while the home is on market
  • Risk of buyer financing falling through

Option 2: List With a Realtor As-Is

Skip the repairs, take a lower price. This works for some properties — but in today's market, buyers are picky. Inherited homes that haven't been updated in 20+ years often sit, attract lowball offers, or only get bites from investors anyway.

Option 3: Sell Directly to a Cash Buyer

This is what Royal Groups Realty does — and it's typically the fastest, cleanest exit for inherited Texas homes. No repairs, no commissions, no showings, no waiting. We've closed deals with executors and heirs in as few as 7 days. Out-of-state heirs love it because everything can be handled remotely.

What If There Are Multiple Heirs Who Don't Agree?

This is one of the most common situations we run into in Texas. Three siblings inherit a house. One wants to sell now, one wants to wait, one wants to keep it as a rental. Things stall, holding costs pile up, and the family relationship gets strained.

A cash sale is often the cleanest path forward here because:

  • The offer is concrete, in writing, and has a fixed timeline — easier to make a group decision
  • No need for everyone to coordinate on repairs, showings, or staging
  • Closing can happen quickly, so everyone can move on
  • Funds are split at closing through the title company according to ownership percentages

We've also handled situations where one heir buys out the others, or where only certain heirs want to sell their share. Read more: Selling a Partial Interest in a Property.

What About Inherited Homes With a Mortgage Still on Them?

Most inherited Texas homes still have an active mortgage. Under federal law (the Garn-St. Germain Act), heirs can typically assume the existing mortgage without it being called due — but you're still on the hook for the monthly payment until you sell.

When we close on an inherited home with a mortgage, the loan is paid off at closing from the sale proceeds. Whatever's left after the mortgage payoff goes to the heirs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you buy an inherited home before probate closes?

Usually yes. We can sign a purchase agreement during probate and close as soon as the executor or administrator has legal authority to transfer the deed. In most Texas counties, that's 30–90 days from filing.

What if the home is in a different city than where I live?

Not a problem. We routinely work with out-of-state heirs. The entire process — from offer to closing — can be done remotely. You don't need to fly to Texas. Documents can be signed digitally or via mobile notary.

What if the house is full of the deceased's belongings?

Leave whatever you don't want. We handle full clean-outs at no cost to you. Many heirs find this aspect alone is worth the cash sale — the emotional weight of sorting through a parent's possessions is one of the hardest parts of the whole process.

Do you work with estate attorneys and executors?

Yes — frequently. If you have an attorney handling the estate, we'll work directly with them on documentation and closing logistics.

What if the property has tax liens or a reverse mortgage?

We handle both regularly. Tax liens are resolved at closing through the title company. Reverse mortgages have their own payoff process — we work with the lender to settle the balance at closing. Read more: Selling a House With Liens.

What if some heirs want to keep the home and others want to sell?

The heirs who want to sell can sell their fractional interests — or one heir can buy the others out. We can help structure either scenario. Read more: Selling a Partial Interest in a Property.

How do you determine the offer price?

We look at the home's condition, location, recent comparable sales in the immediate neighborhood, current Texas market trends, and any repairs or title issues we'll need to handle. We share the full reasoning openly — no black-box algorithms.

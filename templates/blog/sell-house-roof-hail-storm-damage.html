How to Sell a House With Roof, Hail, or Storm Damage

By Luca Rebuffi – Royal Groups Realty  ·  November 2026  ·  8 min read
The Situation

A Damaged Roof Doesn’t Have to Be Your Next Project

One hailstorm can turn a perfectly fine roof into a problem overnight. Maybe you see missing shingles, dented vents, or a brown stain spreading across a bedroom ceiling. Maybe you don’t see anything yet, but a neighbor’s roofer left a flyer on your door saying you do.

Now you’re juggling an insurance claim, roofing quotes, and a house you may have wanted to sell anyway. Repairs cost real money, contractors get booked up after big storms, and a house with an obviously damaged roof tends to sit on the market.

The good news is that you have more than one way out. You can repair first, file a claim, sell as-is, or do some combination. This guide walks through each so you can see what actually ends up in your pocket. We buy houses with roof, hail, and storm damage throughout Texas and Georgia, as-is, for cash, with no repairs and no commissions.

💡 Take dated photos of everything before you do anything else, including the roof, the ceilings, and any water stains. They help with insurance, with disclosure, and with any buyer conversation.

Why It Matters

Why Roof Damage Makes a House Hard to Sell

A roof is one of the first things buyers, inspectors, and lenders look at, and a damaged one causes trouble in several places at once:

  • Financing. Lenders and appraisers look at roof condition. FHA-type loans can require repairs or a roof inspection before closing, so a damaged roof can narrow your buyer pool to people paying cash.
  • Inspections. A roof problem usually shows up on the buyer’s inspection, and buyers tend to respond with big repair requests or a lower price.
  • Hidden damage. A leak can soak insulation, rot decking, and stain ceilings long before it’s obvious. Buyers assume the worst about what they can’t see. Our guide on mold and water damage explains where that can lead.
  • Insurance. Some insurers raise premiums, add roof exclusions, or decline to renew older roofs, which makes buyers wary about getting coverage.
  • Timing. After a major storm, local roofers fill up fast, and repairs can drag on for weeks or months.

Disclosure still applies

Selling as-is doesn’t erase what you know. In Texas, sellers of most previously occupied homes must give buyers a written seller’s disclosure notice, and its structural section covers the roof. If you know about hail damage, a leak, or a past repair, that generally needs to be disclosed, and a claim is evidence that a problem existed. Georgia doesn’t use a single mandatory form, but sellers still have a duty to disclose known latent defects. This is general information, not legal advice, so ask a real estate attorney if you’re unsure how to answer a specific question.

Your Options

What You Can Do With a Storm-Damaged Roof

Option 1: File an insurance claim and repair

If the damage is covered, a claim may pay for part or all of a new roof, minus your deductible. Many policies set a deadline to file after a storm, so check yours early. A few things to know:

  • Texas law makes it illegal for a roofing contractor to offer to waive, rebate, or absorb your deductible, so be careful with anyone who promises a “free roof.”
  • A contractor who will also do the work generally can’t act as your public adjuster.
  • Insurers may pay actual cash value first and hold back a portion until work is done, depending on your policy.
  • Claims can affect your premiums or coverage, so many owners talk to their insurance agent first.

The Texas Department of Insurance publishes plain-English guidance on roofing and insurance, and it’s worth a read before you sign anything with a contractor.

Option 2: Repair, then list

A new roof can widen your buyer pool and remove the biggest inspection issue. But it costs money up front, it takes time to schedule and finish, and then you still have a full listing period ahead of you, with commissions, showings, and the usual risk of a deal falling through.

Option 3: List it as-is on the open market

You can list a damaged house as-is. Expect investors and cash buyers at the table, and expect financed buyers to ask for credits or repairs. If the house is already on the market and not moving, see our guide on what to do when a listing stalls.

Option 4: Sell for cash, as-is

A cash sale means no lender, no appraisal, and no repair requests. The trade-off is price: offers typically land between 70% and 85% of fair market value, depending on condition and repair costs. So the real question is what you net after repairs, commissions, and months of carrying costs. Open claim or unspent insurance money?

If you’ve received insurance money and haven’t used it for repairs, or a claim is still open, tell any buyer up front and ask your insurer how it affects a sale. Your mortgage lender may also be named on claim payments. Because this can involve your policy, your loan, and the sale contract, it’s a good question for your insurance agent and a real estate attorney.
